Know2Act: What Knowledge Reaches
a Tool-Using Agent’s Actions?

Yiwen Lu

University of Notre Dame · ylu37@nd.edu

Manuscript · October 2026

Paper Code BibTeX

Abstract

Tool-using agents are increasingly trained to predict more than their next action: the observations the environment will return, the outcome of a call, or the answer they are looking for, and such predictions are credited with making later reinforcement learning (RL) more effective. Between learning a prediction and acting on it, however, lie three separate steps. The knowledge must be acquired, it must enter the context in which actions are chosen, and the decision rule must use it. Know2Act (from knowledge to action) is a pre-registered study of a multi-turn text-to-SQL agent, trained with supervised fine-tuning and then GRPO, that measures each step on its own. Every prediction we supervise is learned and transfers to unseen databases, from the structure of result tables to an answer contract that states the shape of the correct answer. Whether a prediction reaches the action policy depends on where it is placed: the same outcome line moves the policy only when it is written into the action. The learned contract is usable at once from outside the agent, as a selector among sampled answers that adds to execution-based voting. Yet the agent’s own decision to submit or keep querying does not consult it: holding the model and context fixed, swapping or removing the contract at the decision point changes the check the agent writes next but not whether it submits. RL, which rewards only the final answer, gives no variant an advantage and removes even the selector’s gain. Learned knowledge thus reaches selection before it reaches decisions, and each step needs its own test. We also find that 44% of the training tasks in a public SQL RL environment reward an empty answer.

The question

A tool-using agent issues a query, the environment returns a result, and the agent decides what to do next. Standard supervised fine-tuning puts the loss on the agent’s actions only. ActObs (Zhang et al., 2026) also trains the agent to predict the observations, reports that GRPO then reaches higher pass@k on Terminal-Bench, and attributes the gain to the agent learning what its actions do. For that explanation, or any predictive objective, to hold, three separate things must be true, and a gain measured at the end of training does not say which of them is:

STEP 1
The knowledge is acquired. The model predicts its target better than before, on databases it has never seen.Measured by held-out prediction quality.
STEP 2
It enters the action context. A prediction the model can make but never conditions on cannot change what it writes next.Measured by action KL, against a data-order noise floor.
STEP 3
The decision uses it. An agent that knows what a correct answer looks like should submit when its result has that shape and keep querying when it does not.Measured by submit-or-continue sensitivity, against retraining noise.

Know2Act measures each step on its own, for observation supervision and for four further predictions placed at different points of the trajectory, up to an answer contract that states the shape of the correct answer and is checked against every result. The agent is a Qwen3-4B student trained on Qwen3-32B trajectories in the SkyRL text-to-SQL environment, fine-tuned and then trained with GRPO, and evaluated on Spider-dev and BIRD-dev with 16 samples per question. The study was pre-registered before any main run.

Three findings

1. The knowledge is learned, and it transfers to unseen databases

Every target we supervise is acquired. On the 12 databases that fine-tuning never saw, the observation-supervised model predicts the output template almost perfectly and the result header far better than the action-only model (cross-entropy on the first header tokens 1.02 vs. 5.64 nats); what it learns about row values is memorised from the training databases and does not transfer (1.92 vs. 1.88 nats). The outcome line is learned equally well in both placements. The Contract model states the correct coarse shape of the answer in 89% (Spider) and 82% (BIRD) of its rollouts, against 42% and 60% for always writing the most frequent shape, 71% and 49% for a classifier that reads only the question, and 30% and 35% for a placebo trained on contracts from other questions. Its checks against each result are computed correctly more than 99.7% of the time.

2. Where a prediction is placed decides whether it reaches the actions

Outcome-Aux and Outcome-in-Action supervise the same line, Expected result: 3 rows, 2 columns., with the same weight; one places it after the query as an auxiliary target, the other before the query as part of the action. Only the in-action line changes the actions: its KL to ActionSFT is 2.3 times the data-order noise floor, while the auxiliary line stays inside the noise. Observation supervision, whose targets are never generated at test time, also stays inside the noise (0.0049 against a floor of 0.0070), and its behaviour after errors and empty results is the same as the action-only model’s. Whether knowledge enters the actions is therefore a question of placement, not of how well it is learned.

Observation. The in-action predictions are blind to failure. Outcome-in-Action writes “error” before only 9 of the 8,311 calls that do fail. Its labels come from teacher trajectories in which only 2.8% of calls fail, and predicting one’s own query’s result adds little when running it is free.

3. The learned contract is usable now, as a selector among sampled answers

Knowledge the agent holds can be used before anything else changes. For each question we take the contract the Contract model writes most often across its 16 samples and pick a sample whose final result has that shape. Before RL this beats a filter that only drops errors and empty results by 4.3 (Spider) and 3.4 (BIRD) points, and the placebo’s contracts add only about one point. The contract also transfers to another model’s samples: applied to ActionSFT, which never saw a contract, and combined with majority voting over execution results, it adds 2.6 and 1.4 points to voting alone and comes within half a point of an oracle that knows the gold result’s shape.

Where it stops: the agent’s own decision

A selector can use the contract; the agent itself does not. After every result the agent either submits or keeps querying, and an agent that used answer knowledge would submit when the result has the right shape and keep querying when it does not. We measure the decision sensitivity, how much more often the agent submits after a result with the gold shape than after one without. ActionSFT already shows a large asymmetry (0.43 on Spider, 0.46 on BIRD), inherited from the teacher, and no variant changes it by more than retraining ActionSFT on a reshuffled data order does (at most 0.016 against a noise reference of 0.033 on Spider; 0.010 against 0.020 on BIRD). This holds for the Contract model, whose own check names every mismatch in words: it submits after 55% and 31% of the mismatches it reports, the same rates as ActionSFT after mismatches judged against the gold shape.

The direct test: swap the contract at the decision point

The comparison above is between models. To test whether the decision consults the contract, we hold the model and its context fixed and change only the contract. From the Contract model’s own prefix up to its first SQL result, it continues four times under each condition, with the same random numbers: its own contract, a contract under which the same result flips between match and mismatch, no contract at all, and a forced next check sentence that agrees or disagrees. The contract is read: the check the model writes next follows the new contract in 99.4–100% of continuations. It is not consulted: the probability of submitting moves by at most 0.008, while on the same decision points the result’s own shape moves it by 0.19–0.54. The decision follows the result, not what the agent’s contract or check says about it.

The outcome reward does not close the gap. After the same GRPO from every initialisation, observation supervision and both outcome lines reach the same pooled pass@16 as the action-only model (ActObs minus ActionSFT +0.26 points, 95% CI [−0.38, +0.91], averaged over two RL seeds each; Contract −0.06 [−0.78, +0.66]; Outcome-in-Action, the only variant whose prediction moves the policy, −0.06 [−0.82, +0.68]), the contract no longer helps a selector, and what RL does change is shared by every variant: it makes the agent more willing to submit after a mismatching result. Measured with Contract’s own checks on BIRD, submitting after a reported mismatch rises from 31% to 46%.

Observation. Training itself is noisy. Two RL seeds of the same ActObs checkpoint end 0.97 points apart on Spider pass@1, and two checkpoints that act the same before RL (ActionSFT-LR and ActObs) end 1.48 points apart on BIRD pass@1. With variance of this size, an effect as small as the one ActObs reports for a 4B model (+1.1 pass@16) would go undetected here.
What this means. Learning, entering and using are separate claims, and a gain at the end of training does not say which of them holds. Here the first two hold and the last one fails: the contract is correct, sits in the agent’s context and is read when the agent writes its check, yet the submit-or-retry decision does not consult it. Making knowledge reach decisions probably requires rewarding its use, or training on the agent’s own failure-rich interaction, rather than adding another prediction target.

Training variants

All variants train on the same 24,043 teacher trajectories, in the same order and with the same hyper-parameters, and then go through the same GRPO. They differ only in which tokens carry loss or in a short line inserted into the text. The main study was pre-registered, and the predictions for the later variants were written down before they were trained.

The prediction written before each call ignores what the call will do: the first call fails and the second returns 5 rows, yet both predictions say 10 rows.

Environment audit

The public SkyRL text-to-SQL environment rewards a solution when its result set equals the gold query’s. Running the unmodified environment with the constant answer SELECT 1 WHERE 0; earns full reward on 290 of its 653 training tasks (44%): 283 of the 540 SynSQL tasks and 7 of the 113 Spider tasks. Their gold queries return no rows, and for 18 of them the result depends on the system clock through filters such as year > strftime('%Y','now') - 5. We train RL only on the 363 tasks with non-empty gold results, and reported the problem to the maintainers with a reproduction script (SkyRL issue #2451).
